FIELD NOTES / UPDATED 4 OCT 2026

Chasing the signs of life

Sun-followers, comet-like blobs, worms and labyrinths: the promising moments are shaping the next experiments. We’re looking for what makes those behaviors possible, then trying to amplify them. These notes keep observations, measured results and ambitions distinct.

THE NEW DIRECTION

Find the spark. Turn it up.

Our strongest working hunch is that reopening resource-rich space helps keep worlds interesting. Moving light also gives sensing and movement a purpose; selection among ponds gives us a way to push a chosen trait. Starting genomes and time matter too.

We want to search across combinations and strengths, with a tenfold increase in a compelling trait as an ambition. Ten times the disturbance does not mean ten times the life: harsh wounds can favor thin films, and fast-moving light can leave founders behind.

The first storm-world sandbox look makes that tradeoff concrete. Its full stack went extinct in one seed; slower sunlight was a more promising lead. The breeder is also being developed in that separate sandbox. These sandbox features are not yet part of the public lab.

PROMISING / EXPLORATORY

The observations we’re following

  • Sun-followers. Hand-built sensing patterns survived wandering light where blind drifters failed in a small comparison. A lead about environment and sensing, not an evolved capability.
  • Comet-like blobs. Noticed in repeatedly reset random-donor ponds. Visually promising; speed was not measured.
  • Worms and labyrinths. Recurring wounds and inherited shape rules produced longer, more elaborate bodies in a few runs. The useful range of disturbance still needs exploring.
  • Selection among ponds. Choosing which groups seed the next cycle provides a practical starting point for breeding toward visible traits. More drive in a score must still be checked against actual movement.

MET · 27 SEP

Regenerating founders

The M3 screen found genetically distinct seeds that regrow after a 30% lesion and die in a no-light control. A stricter retest narrowed the founder set to 12 genomes, which seeded the long-run ensemble.

Passing this gate does not establish open-ended evolution.

NOT MET · 28 SEP

Open-ended evolution (M4)

All 170 registered runs of 10⁶ steps finished with exact conservation. With mutation, new adaptive activity exceeded both controls in one of the two worlds. The second test failed in both: neutral runs, where mutations do not change behaviour, also counted as “growing”. A 10⁷-step extension showed the same pattern.

Biotic recycling and coexisting roles held. The held-out hypothesis was not supported (0 of 4 observables).

FINDING · 29 SEP

Where variation stalls

Genomes are copied faithfully: grown separately, the genomes of tracked parents and offspring behave alike (slopes of 0.85–0.96), though most pairs carried identical genomes. Mutation rarely changes what a genome does in the ecosystem: the mutants of only 1 of the 12 founders reliably took up a new role. A broader sample of genomes did so far more often (11 of 24), so the way founders were chosen was a bottleneck.

Evolved lineages also lost the founders’ ability to regenerate (0.17 against 0.98).

OPEN · 29 SEP

What counts as an individual

The tracker credits each birth to a nearby parent. A direct check found that in 135 of 200 sampled births the credited parent contributed no copied material at the time of the birth. A tracked birth is therefore not, by itself, evidence that an individual reproduced.

This is a limit of the measurement, not a negative result. It qualifies every result built on tracked births. New exploratory worlds can still be tried while better measurements are developed.

BASELINE · 1 OCT

Measures that need work

None of the four activity, novelty and role measures met every validation requirement: three flagged too many neutral runs, and the role measure barely separated the comparison it was meant to.

This limits what those scores can tell us about evolution. A visually interesting world remains a lead to investigate.

EXPLORATORY · 30 SEP

Founders and environments

What the founders were mattered more than how varied they were. Clouds of drifted founder genomes did little better than single founders (2 of 12 against 1 of 12), while a set made only of non-producing genomes met the new-role criterion in 4 of 5 runs, though part of that may be sorting of roles already present. Whether new roles keep arriving after niches first fill is mixed: they do not in the main comparison, and an excess in the 10⁷-step extension becomes unclear once ambiguous returns are set aside.

A bug that zeroed a background genome’s weights in one screen was found and fixed; the affected result is corrected in the plan.

REPLICATED · 1 OCT

A life cycle for groups

Following experiments that impose a group life cycle on microbes (Black, Bourrat & Rainey, 2020), each tile became a pond. At every cycle, ponds are cleared and reseeded with a small fragment from a successful pond. In new histories, with each test fixed before its data, heritable differences between ponds (genetic or structural) appeared in 6 of 6. Fragments from cycled ponds also passed a regrowth test more often than those from random-donor ponds, uncycled ponds and the ancestor, both at once and after 200,000 steps without the cycle, again in 6 of 6.

Exploratory, not a registered milestone. Random-donor ponds show heritable differences too, so the cycle’s causal role is not shown. The test of how much of the advantage the genome carries passed only at its threshold. The life cycle is imposed rather than discovered.

METHOD

Checks before claims

Runs are compared across conditions and seeds. Conservation checks, CPU/GPU golden tests and replay verification catch implementation drift. For confirmatory tests, decisions are written down before the data they depend on. Exploratory searches can follow what looks interesting, with those readings labelled as such. A failed test stays recorded as failed.
