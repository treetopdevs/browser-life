Life from noise

Programs that learn to copy themselves

Every square on the grid is a 64-byte program, random at the start. Each step, neighbours pair up: their bytes are joined into one tape, run as a tiny self-editing program, and split back. Nothing rewards copying and nothing declares a birth. Sooner or later, a program that happens to copy itself into its partner appears, and its copies spread across the grid.

A port of Google's cubff, the code behind Computational Life (Agüera y Arcas et al., 2024), checked byte for byte against its published numbers. The wait is long and luck-dependent: in our runs on the paper's 240 × 135 grid, one of two seeds produced copiers, after about 17,000 steps. "Plant copiers here" skips the wait.

How to read this view
Colour marks copies.

      

        
In the family view, a random soup is dark: almost every program is one of a kind. When copiers take over, a coloured patch grows outward from where they started. Each family has its own colour, picked at random from its instructions; a mutation that changes an instruction makes a new family with an unrelated colour, so a population of competing variants looks speckled, and a variant that wins turns its region one colour.

        
The byte view draws what the paper draws: every program as an 8 × 8 block of its bytes. It shows the most detail on a large screen, where copies of one program appear as a shared texture.

        
Each program only ever meets programs within two squares of it, so families spread as fronts instead of mixing instantly. Mutation keeps throwing in random bytes; copiers survive it by copying faster than it erodes them.
