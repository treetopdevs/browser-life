A different chemistry · life from noise

Programs that learn to copy themselves.

Every square on the grid is a 64-byte program, random at the start. Each step, neighbours pair up: their bytes are joined into one tape, run as a tiny self-editing program, and split back. Nothing rewards copying and nothing declares a birth. Sooner or later a program that happens to copy itself into its partner appears, and its copies spread across the grid.

The wait is long and depends on luck. On the paper’s 240 × 135 grid, one of our two seeds grew copiers, after about 17,000 steps; the other did not in the time we ran it. To skip the wait, click a square and press Plant copiers here.

How to read it

        

          
Colour marks copies.

          
In the family view a random soup is dark: almost every program is one of a kind. When copiers take over, a coloured patch grows outward from where they started. Each family has its own colour, picked at random from its instructions. A mutation that changes an instruction makes a new family with an unrelated colour, so competing variants look speckled, and a variant that wins turns its region one colour.

          
The byte view draws what the paper draws: every program as an 8 × 8 block of its bytes. It shows the most on a large screen, where copies of one program appear as a shared texture.

          
Each program only ever meets programs within two squares of it, so families spread as fronts instead of mixing at once. Mutation keeps throwing in random bytes; copiers survive it by copying faster than it wears them down.

        

      

      

        
Where it comes from

        

          
A port of a published experiment.

          
This is Google’s cubff, the code behind Computational Life (Agüera y Arcas and colleagues, 2024), ported to run in your browser and checked byte for byte against the original. It is not the lab’s chemistry: there is no matter, light or energy here, only programs rewriting each other. We keep it beside the jars because it asks the same question from the other end: what does it take for copying to start?

          
Without WebGPU the page falls back to a smaller 120 × 68 grid on the processor, which is much slower.
