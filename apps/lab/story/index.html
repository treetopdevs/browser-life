The story so far · 26 Sep to 26 Nov 2026

Trying to grow something nobody drew.

Two months of building small sealed worlds and watching what lives in them. This page is about the making: what we are after, how far each world got, the times we stopped and changed course, and how the work gets done. Most of the answers so far are “no” or “we can’t tell yet”.

What we are after

Creatures that evolve into things nobody scripted

The thing we want to see is easy to say. Creatures you can watch, that reproduce and evolve on their own into forms nobody designed, in a world that can run for a long time and be shared. It is what the game Spore seemed to promise, and what a lifetime of cellular automata keeps hinting at.

That is a long way off, so we cut it into rungs. Each rung is something you could see with your own eyes if it held. The work goes on the lowest rung that does not hold yet, in one world at a time, and a new world is only allowed when a rung proves out of reach in the old one. That rule is recent. Most of this page is the story of how we came to need it.

  1. 1
    Individuals that divide and dieSeen · seeds 1 and 2

    Bodies you can point at, that make more bodies and stop existing, without the program declaring what a body is.

    Bodies are groups of bonded cells, found each step by walking the bonds. They grow, let daughters go, starve and break.

  2. 2
    HereditySeen · seeds 1 and 2

    A daughter is like her mother because something was copied, and the copy can differ.

    Each cell carries genes and a growth plan, copied with occasional errors.

  3. 3
    Selection between individualsSeen · seeds 1 and 2

    Some bodies leave more descendants because of what they inherited.

    Bodies that chase food displaced sitters and wanderers on both seeds. Chasing was a dial we wrote, so this is the least we could ask.

  4. 4
    Heredity that can inventA first versionWe are here

    Genes whose effects are not picked from a list we wrote, so evolution can make a form nobody drew.

    Growth plans are a first version. One clearly new outline, a two-wide strip we call the ladder, arose from one changed plan entry and took over on one seed. It did not on the other.

  5. 5
    Creatures that differ from their ancestors, and between runsNot yet

    Run it twice and get two different worlds, each full of things the founders were not.

    Seen once each way, on two seeds, and only under a size rule and a size limit that we wrote. Most of the many outlines are one plan grown crooked.

  6. 6
    It keeps goingNot yet

    Arms races and new ways of making a living that keep arriving, with no hand on the dial.

    Armour and hunters swung one way and back in one history. A later change that shifted the histories erased it, and we withdrew it.

The rungs as they stand in Roam and Soft Roam, the world we are working in, on 26 Nov 2026. “Seen” means watched on seeds 1 and 2, the ones every setting was tuned on. Nothing on this ladder has been confirmed on seeds we have not looked at.

How far each world got

Seven kinds of world, and where each one stopped

Laid side by side, the worlds show the plot of the last two months. The ones with rich heredity never had a body that held up. The ones with real bodies gave heredity only a few dials to turn. The world we are in now is the attempt to have both.

    Now: Where we are. Everything in it is seen on two seeds and not yet confirmed on seeds we have not looked at.

The pips are our reading of the results further down, not a result themselves. A world that we wrote the individuals into gets no credit for having them.

How it gets made

Ask, build small, count, look

Most of the typing here is done by AI coding agents. A person decides what is worth trying and whether what came back is interesting. Agents write the worlds, run them and draft pages like this one, and as a rule a second model, from a different maker, reads a change before it is kept. We say so plainly because it shapes everything else. Agents are quick and tireless, and they will cheerfully build a beautiful instrument that measures nothing. Most of our habits exist to catch that.

One round of work

  1. 1A sentence first. Before anything is built: if this works completely, you will see what? If the answer is a number on a dial we wrote ourselves, it is not worth building.
  2. 2A small world, the same day. Each line of work gets its own copy of the code, so several can run side by side and a dead end costs a day.
  3. 3Count everything. Matter is counted exactly in every world: what goes in equals what comes out, to the unit, at every step. In the jar and in the Roam worlds all the arithmetic is in whole numbers as well, so the same seed replays bit for bit on another machine. That is what lets a stranger’s browser check a run of ours.
  4. 4A second reader. Another model reviews the change before it lands, and the notes say so when a last fix went in unread. In Roam it twice found that bodies earlier in the program’s scan order were being quietly favoured, which no test of ours had caught. When a number matters, a third pass works it out again by a different route.
  5. 5Look at it. The new thing goes on a page and somebody watches it. Whether it is interesting is decided by eye. Whether it is true is decided separately.
  6. 6Write it down with a label. Every result goes in the journal with a word that says how sure we are, and what we saw, what we measured and what we hope are kept apart.
  7. 7Believe it once. Exploring is loose: two seeds, a paragraph of intent, a day. Believing is strict: the rules frozen first, seeds nobody has looked at, one run.

What goes wrong

Three things, over and over. The instruments lie: our own birth counter credited parents that had copied nothing, and it took a direct check to find out. The machinery lies: one graphics card silently dropped work in long runs, so those histories are marked as not exact. And we fool ourselves, in two opposite ways. One is getting excited by a short run on the seeds a world was tuned on. The other is its cure taken too far: a small question grows a long plan, frozen rules and a list of named outcomes before anyone has looked at the thing.

So there are tripwires now. We stop and look at the whole when two results in a row are statistics and not something visible, when somebody proposes a follow-up to a follow-up, when a week passes with nothing new to watch, or when a one-day look runs past its day. The last time one tripped was in Roam, three changes deep into making armour and hunters swing.

Before you start

How to read the rest

Three acts, in the order things happened, with the points where we stopped and changed course marked as steps back. We are learning in public, so the misses are shown as plainly as the hits. Every result carries a label. Confirmed is kept for tests whose rules were frozen before their data. Negative means a test with pre-stated rules came back no. Measured and Exploratory are real numbers from a few seeds, with no claim beyond them, though some of those tests also fixed their rules before running. Open and Unclear mean the test could not tell, which is different from a no.

Each chapter folds its results away under it. The full write-up of every one is in the journal, and the experiments index lists them all with their sources. Three of the worlds can be grown right on this page, and the prototypes each have a page of their own. Nothing here is evidence of open-ended evolution, the kind that keeps producing new things without end. That is the thing we are looking for, not a thing we have found.

Act one · 26 Sep to 16 Nov

A rich genome and nobody home

For seven weeks the work was one sealed jar and the instruments pointed at it. Every cell in it runs a small evolved controller, which is heredity with plenty of room. What we could not find in it was an individual that held up.

1 · The jar

Can rules this simple grow something that mends itself?

The main world here is a sealed jar: a grid of cells holding nutrient, biomass, membrane and waste. Light comes in; nothing else does. All the arithmetic is in whole numbers, so the lab can check at every step that not one unit of matter was made or lost, and replay any run bit for bit.

The program never says where an organism begins or ends. There is no “organism” in the code and no “reproduce” instruction. Bodies, births and families are things we count afterwards, from what the cells did. That choice matters for everything below. Some of the smaller worlds in chapter 6 break these rules on purpose: they supply bodies, reproduction or outside food, and say so.

The first question was modest: can such a world hold a pattern that grows back after we cut it? A search found seeds that regrow after a wound removes about 30% of a body, and that die when the light is switched off. Twelve of them became the founders for everything in the next chapter.

The results behind this chapter
  • Confirmed · 27 Sep

    Regenerating founders A screen found genetically distinct seeds that regrow after a wound removes about 30% of a body, and die without light. Passing this gate does not establish open-ended evolution.

Things that mend themselves are a start. The harder question is what they do when left alone for a long time.

2 · The long run

Left alone, does it keep evolving?

We wrote the test down first, then ran it: 170 runs of a million steps each, with the founders from chapter 1, in two worlds. Every run kept its matter exactly. The milestone was not met. Runs with mutation did show more new adaptive activity than both controls in one of the two worlds. But a second test failed in both: neutral runs, where mutations change nothing about behaviour, also counted as “growing”. So one world showed more adaptive activity, but a growth test that neutral runs also pass cannot establish continued evolution.

Looking closer told us where it stalls. Genomes are copied faithfully, but mutation rarely changes what a genome does: the mutants of only 1 of the 12 founders reliably took up a new role, where a broader sample of genomes did so 11 times in 24. The way we had chosen founders was a bottleneck. Evolved lines had also lost the founders’ knack for regrowing.

Two later checks closed side doors. A search for habitats that keep themselves going found none in its 24 pilot cases. And one mutant that had seemed to regrow better than its founder, tested again with fresh rules, tied it: 28 recoveries in 32 trials each, with four wins either way.

A step back · 28 Sep

The long run was meant to be followed by a bigger one. It did not pass, so we did not scale up.

The next days went to reviewing the foundations instead, and to a written reassessment of every open thread: founders, environment, physics, or a different unit of reproduction. The rest of this act is those four threads being pulled.

A null that “grows” is a problem with the ruler as much as with the world. So the next question was about the ruler.

3 · The instruments

Were our instruments seeing what we thought?

Often, no. Our tracker credits each birth to a nearby parent. When we checked directly, in 135 of 200 sampled births the credited parent had contributed no copied material at the time of the birth. A tracked birth is not, by itself, evidence that anything reproduced. That qualifies every result built on tracked births, including some of our own.

The scores we used for activity, novelty and ecological role fared no better: none of the four met every validation requirement, and three flagged too many neutral runs. Three newer measures, read from the family tree of mutations, failed as well. In one world every one of them ranked the neutral runs as more “selective” than the real ones.

The habit this taught us: before pointing a measure at a world, point it at something where the answer is known. A movie with nine copies of one glider should not score as nine times more interesting than one glider; a cheap error count says it is. A naive score for “built from reused parts” rated duplicated random shapes above a hand-built hierarchy every time.

A step back · 4 Oct

A change of posture, toward bigger bets than a careful scientist would take. The point is to see something alive-looking that nobody drew.

From here the work would follow behaviours that look promising, combine environmental pressures and breed toward traits you can see. Everything still gets counted exactly. That part did not change.

4 · A hand on the dial

Can we turn it up by hand?

Yes, when we do the selecting. We split a world into ponds, and at each cycle cleared them and reseeded from a small fragment of a successful pond, following experiments that impose a life cycle on groups of microbes. Then we put it through a registered test: rules frozen first, 24 histories per arm. Both primary hypotheses were confirmed. Worlds that had lived under the cycle founded new ponds from fragments more often than the controls did, and still did 200,000 steps after the cycle stopped. It was one of only two results labelled Confirmed in these seven weeks; the other is the founders of chapter 1. The catch is in the first sentence: the life cycle is ours. Whether ponds can select themselves is prepared and has not run.

Then we gave genomes a gene they could gain. The first, decomposition, swept to about 99% of living cells in all three seeds. It paid for its lineage and not for the world: those worlds ended as a thin decomposing film. A second gene, for building, taught us about controls instead. A copy that cost the same and did nothing reached about half the living cells in two of three control seeds, so the real gene’s one good seed said nothing. Forcing carriers to build gave an unclear result: the paid gene stayed at or under 1% of living cells whether it built or not. Two screens for a world where building pays, the second over seven worlds and 103 runs, found none that passed.

A lot of what we see in these chapters is something taking over. Taking over is easy. It is not the same as getting better, and it is often bad for the world it happens in.

5 · A second pair of eyes

Can a machine notice for us?

Not yet. We want a naturalist in the garden: something that rings a bell when a world does something worth a look. The first bell was plain code that scores how far a run has strayed from neutral runs of the same world. It rang on 30 of 70 neutral runs, where its bar allowed at most 2. It was ringing on drift. A second version, with its two noisiest signals removed, rang on none of 70 new neutral runs. But given twin worlds that differ by one deliberate wound, it rang on 4 of 10 for the largest wound, where its rule needed 8 of 10. It can tell kinds of run apart. It cannot yet hear something happen inside one.

We tried an image model on a case where we knew the answer, a takeover in the program soup from the next chapter. It passed, thinly, and it also reacted to a blip in the control. A rise in its score means “something visibly changed”, not “life happened”.

And we asked whether a simple rule could predict what a nudge would do to a world before we tried it. On held-out cases it was wrong 73 times in 96, and scored worse than a baseline that ignores the evidence.

6 · Other worlds

Is it the world’s fault?

So we built other worlds, each small enough to answer one question, and most of them are on this site to run.

Food. In a prototype world of bodies run by small programs, we changed only how food arrives. Even food kept three seeds alive and changing. A band of food that moves and one that stays put both passed, so the screen could not tell them apart. Alternating bands gave a seasonal pattern once, and not again on the next seed. Run the food screens.

Depth. A thin 3D slab was meant to hold a sparse living world that shades its own floor. Sixty settings on three seeds each: none qualified. Life stayed sparse, died out, or carpeted the slab. Eighty more settings the next day gave the same answer, and we closed that line. A side idea, three kinds that eat each other in a ring, stopped at its first gate. On 15 Nov we reopened depth, as a taller jar and as a mycelium world you can turn. So far the jar has given a nearly empty world, a lid or thick mats, and on twelve fresh seeds the mycelium world gave no world of bodies. These were screens and looks by eye, not tests. See the slabs and the jar.

Programs. In a soup of random 64-byte programs that run each other, self-copiers arose and took over in 3 of 8 well-mixed soups. After takeover the leading copier keeps being replaced. Races between leaders settled only 19 of 63 pairings, so whether the replacements are improvements is not shown. The long soups behind those leaders ran on a GPU we later found can silently drop work, so their histories are not exact runs of the published soup. Watch the soup.

Brains. In the mycelium world, particles carry small neural controllers, lay a chemical trail and split. Whether inheriting a controller matters could not be measured. A sharper question could: when laying trail only costs, inheritance drops it, and a toll rule that makes moving cheaper for trail-layers keeps it. But the controllers were not seen to follow the trail’s gradient; whether they read its level is untested. Open the mycelium world.

Company. The shared garden is one big stormy world grown in turns by whoever is tending it. It is open: visitors can drop a stone in it or send a sun. For it we asked how long a visitor’s mark stays findable in a storm. Our measure saturated, so that answer is unclear.

7 · Sharper questions

What exactly are we asking?

A step back · 1 Nov

We stopped and wrote a plan: fourteen ideas, each one a narrower question with its own controls.

The first small tests of eight of them ran over the next two days, on small worlds, with rules written before the data wherever we could.

Most came back with the same answer, and it was not yes or no. It was: this test cannot tell. Do later genomes beat earlier ones? Nothing shows improvement against the past, and that is not a finding of no improvement, because two of three archives failed a control. One of them looked strongly like progress until its position control, which checks whether the side a genome starts on matters, went over its allowed tolerance. Do small edits to a controller stay local? Too few edits improved anything to say. Does what one body builds help another? Builders left too little behind for the test to detect even a hand-built wall, so it could not tell. Do bodies that cluster follow a moving sun better? The test’s noise floor was above the limit we had set.

That sounds like failure and we do not read it that way. Most of these tests carried a control with a known answer, and where that control failed we did not read the numbers as results.

Act two · 17 to 23 Nov

Bodies with only dials to inherit

A week of worlds built for one purpose: to have bodies. Some of them got there. Each time, what a body could pass on turned out to be a handful of numbers we had chosen.

8 · A body of its own

Can a world hold real individuals?

We started by writing bodies by hand. A growth rule, a signal that tells a cell how far it is from the middle, and a gene for size: alone in a well-fed world, that holds a clean disc and regrows it after a wound. Put several together and they run into one mat. We gave cells heritable recognition marks and let them refuse to grow against a stranger, and the mat did break up, into about a dozen pieces. But the pieces were families, not bodies, and once the world was full they froze. See the bodies.

So we borrowed from how real cells manage it. In the vesicle world a wall is conserved matter under tension, the contents push on it, and division is a shape giving way, not an instruction. These were the first individuals here that nobody declared and that held up: cells grow, pinch in two and burst. With heredity carried in the wall, cells held and kept dividing in 4 of 4 worlds. Then a control spoiled the headline: with heredity in the contents, copied only at the wall, cells held in 4 of 4 worlds too at the same slow mutation rate, so the wall is not shown to be the cause. And when we gave the genes two jobs to divide between them, all 8 worlds ended with one generalist that did both. Open the vesicle world.

A step back · 21 Nov

By now there were two more worlds: droplets that separate out of a chemical mix and are preyed on by a phage, and a reef of small bodies that graze. Both looked good. So had many things before them.

The note we wrote before running says: “Every headline so far was a short pilot on the seeds it was tuned on.” We froze both worlds’ code, wrote the pass rule down, and ran each once on eight seeds nobody had looked at.

Both passed. Then a closer look, the same day, took something back: what the droplet test had counted as droplets were patches in a travelling wave of wall, not compartments that hold together. The test passed and the name was wrong. We reworded the pages and moved to the version of that world where compartments do hold. Watch the wave patches.

In droplet genes, a compartment carries gene templates, and wall is made only where every kind of gene has copies nearby. A parasite, a template that copies twice as fast and does nothing, arises now and then and takes the room the genes need. What decides the outcome is whether copies can leak from one compartment to the next. When the walls keep them in, a parasite ruins only the compartment it arose in, and that compartment loses to its neighbours. In the recorded runs, worlds whose walls keep the copies in held both genes on 4 of 4 seeds, and leaky worlds on none. That held again on eight fresh seeds, and a heritable trait built on it was confirmed on eight more, one of three results on this page that carry that word. Linking genes onto one template, which we expected to help, did not.

And then we watched it for a while, which is the test that matters here. The compartments split and die, the genes hold, and after a few thousand steps nothing more happens that you would call new. The one thing that could evolve was a dial with five settings that we had written. We tried three ways to give these compartments more to do, a bigger body, a heading, and chasing fuel, and all three lost to an arena full of small dots that never had to move.

Act three · 23 Nov to now

A body that has to go to its food

One world, climbed one rung at a time, with something new to watch every day. It is the first world here whose inhabitants move like creatures. Nothing in it is confirmed yet.

9 · Going to the food

What if a body has to go to its food?

Roam’s rules fit in a breath. Cells sit on a grid that wraps round, and hold matter. A bond joins a cell only to a daughter it made, so bodies never fuse. A body pays upkeep for every side it shows to the world, moves as one piece, and has to get to food that falls in patches somewhere else. Nothing declares a body: at every step the bonded groups are found again by walking the bonds. Matter is closed and counted, as everywhere here.

The sentence we wrote before building it was: you will see bodies travel between food patches and leave them empty behind, some arriving first and some starving. On the two seeds we ran, that is what happened. Bodies that chase food displaced bodies that sit and bodies that wander. That is rungs one to three, in a world we can watch, on two seeds.

The same day we tried the next rung. Each cell got a growth plan: sixteen small entries that say where its daughter goes, whether she stays joined or is let go, and which entry comes next. Copy errors change one entry at a time. Starting from plain blocks, on one seed a new outline arose from a single changed entry, a strip two cells wide and sixteen long, and by step 80,000 the world was twenty or so of them and their young. We called it the ladder. On the other seed nothing of the kind had happened by step 150,000. And when we grew each surviving plan alone to see what it really made, most of the variety on screen turned out to be one plan grown crooked.

Next, matter was made to seep from cell to cell along the bonds, and cells to die one at a time, so that bodies break. Then cells came in kinds. A plan can make a shell, cheap to keep and hard to eat, or a mouth, the only cell that feeds on other bodies. Hunters and grazers lived side by side on both seeds. For a while the two seeds went different ways, armour on one and hunters on the other, and when we let shells crack under attack, armour and hunters swung one way and back. We were pleased for about an evening. Then an unrelated change shifted every history and both vanished: the armoured world had come from one lucky early copying error. We withdrew them, wrote a test with its bar stated first, and it failed. One seed passed at 4 of 5 settings and the other at none.

Roam’s bodies hop from tile to tile, which reads as a board game more than as life. Soft Roam keeps every rule and takes the bodies off the grid: a cell has a position and a speed, bonds are springs, and a body glides, bends and turns. Still whole numbers, still exact. Since then it has gained a sun that sweeps round each day and a year that swings from lean to rich, green cells that live on light, hunters that see prey and prey that flee, plans for bodies of twenty-odd cells, and bodies that heal when a cell is lost.

What is there, on the seeds we tuned on: the year is visible, and the number of cells rises and falls with it by half again or more. Cells living in bodies of ten or more went from almost none to about three in ten. Take sight away and, on the one seed where we tried it, hunters caught less than half as much. What is not there: a hunter visibly curving after its prey, bodies that reach the size their plans ask for, or kinds of body whose speeds stay apart. That last one was a bar we set beforehand, and it failed.

Soft Roam’s own page has every dial, and two newer pieces from 27 Nov: a world three levels deep, where most life stays on the floor, and an inner pulse that beats with no clock, though nothing yet says the beat helps a body.

This is the part of the page that is unfinished on purpose. None of what these two jars show has been run on seeds nobody has looked at. We put that check off because the rules were still changing, and it is owed.

Where that leaves us

What we think now

These are readings of the results above, not results themselves.

  • The bookkeeping holds. Registered runs conserved matter exactly, and the replay checks we ran matched bit for bit. Exact bookkeeping does not make a measure valid, as chapter 3 shows.
  • Selection we impose works. Selection a world does for itself is thin so far. The pond cycle is a confirmed, registered result, and the cycle is ours. The one confirmed case of a world doing its own selecting is a trait held between droplet compartments, on a dial of five settings that we wrote.
  • Taking over is cheap. A gene, a copier or a family sweeps a world readily. None of our tests has yet shown a sweep that is an improvement for the world it happens in.
  • Rich heredity and real bodies have not yet met in one world. That is the gap the ladder’s fourth rung names, and Roam’s growth plans are the first thing here that reaches for it.
  • A passed test can be about the wrong thing. The droplet confirmation passed on eight unseen seeds and its droplets were not droplets. Frozen rules protect against wishful reading, not against a wrong name.
  • Watching is an instrument too. About as many of our tests have ended at “cannot tell” as at “no”. And the droplet world, which passed every test we froze for it, was set aside once we had simply watched it for long enough.

What is next

  • Roam’s first three rungs, on seeds nobody has looked at, with the rule written first. Until that runs, the bottom of the ladder is seen and not believed.
  • The fourth rung: growth plans that make a form we did not plant, on more than one seed.
  • Left open on the way, and still open: ponds that select themselves with our hand off the ranking (prepared, not run), a third anomaly bell (proposed, not run), and the soup’s test of whether later copiers do better against copiers they have never met (rules frozen, no result reported).
