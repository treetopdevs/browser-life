Walls made of oligomers

Compartments that fight over wall patterns

Light makes two kinds of small building block out of loose 0s and 1s. Each square of the grid can hold a compartment, a pocket with a wall and a small genome of catalysts. A wall is built from one of six four-letter patterns made of two 0s and two 1s (0011, 0101, 0110, 1001, 1010, 1100). Everything is counted in whole pieces, and the total number of 0s and the total number of 1s never change; the badge below checks that every few frames.

In the worlds with predators, a compartment can evolve a lysin: a catalyst that cuts one wall pattern in its neighbours. The idea being tested is that a pattern which spreads becomes an easy target, and then another pattern takes over. Watch the colour of the grid and the stacked chart: does the leader change? Founders start with no lysin, so the predators arrive only after mutation finds one, which can take thousands of steps.

Designed ingredients: the light that makes food, the six wall patterns, the size a wall must reach before a compartment divides, the lysin rule, background rupture and drift are all engineered, and a compartment is a grid square that is switched on or off. What is not written in is which pattern wins, or which lysins appear.

What this has shown so far: a provisional 40,000-step pilot (4 seeds, 24x24 grid, chosen after three mechanical fixes found during the pilot). With predators, the leading wall pattern changed hands 0 to 12 times in the last 20,000 steps depending on the seed; none of the 12 control runs changed leader. That is a pilot, not the 500,000-step confirmation the plan calls for (not yet run), the leader was checked every 500 steps there and every 200 here, and a run you watch can stay quiet for a long time or show nothing at all.

Known limits: the grid is flat and wraps around at the edges, matter only moves between neighbours, compartments drift by swapping places with a neighbour, and the viewer shows the leading pattern of each square, so a square that is half one pattern and half another looks like the larger half. Squares are not tracked as individuals: the inspector reads the square, not the cell that happens to sit in it. Death counts are observations, not causes: a death with no neighbour cut can be hunger, thin walls or random rupture, and a death with a neighbour cut means a lysin cut a neighbour during that step. The predator ring marks carriers of a lysin gene, not the cuts themselves. Results above are provisional.
