Walls made of oligomers

Compartments that fight over wall patterns

Light makes two kinds of small building block out of loose 0s and 1s. Each square of the grid can hold a compartment, a pocket with a wall and a small genome of catalysts. A wall is built from one of six four-letter patterns made of two 0s and two 1s (0011, 0101, 0110, 1001, 1010, 1100). Everything is counted in whole pieces, and the total number of 0s and the total number of 1s never change; the badge below checks that every few frames.

In the worlds with predators, a compartment can evolve a lysin: a catalyst that cuts one wall pattern in its neighbours. The idea being tested is that a pattern which spreads becomes an easy target, and then another pattern takes over. Watch the colour of the grid and the stacked chart: does the leader change? Founders start with no lysin, so the predators arrive only after mutation finds one, which can take thousands of steps.

Designed ingredients: the light that makes food, the six wall patterns, the size a wall must reach before a compartment divides, the lysin rule, background rupture and drift are all engineered, and a compartment is a grid square that is switched on or off. What is not written in is which pattern wins, or which lysins appear.

What this has shown so far: a provisional 40,000-step pilot (4 seeds, 24x24 grid, chosen after three mechanical fixes found during the pilot). With predators, the leading wall pattern changed hands 0 to 12 times in the last 20,000 steps depending on the seed; none of the 12 control runs changed leader. That is a pilot, not the 500,000-step confirmation the plan calls for (not yet run), the leader was checked every 500 steps there and every 200 here, and a run you watch can stay quiet for a long time or show nothing at all.

Walls made of oligomers · wall-patch worlds

Six wall patterns that fight over one dish

Nothing here is switched on or off. Every square of the grid holds loose pieces, light-made food, six kinds of wall matter (six wall patterns; the names and colours here are display labels only, the rules treat them as six interchangeable kinds) and six kinds of phage. Wall matter of one pattern pushes the other patterns away, so each pattern holds its own regions of thin wall, and waves of thicker wall move through those regions. Nothing is declared: there is no alive flag and no split rule. The patch count on this page is read afterwards by a program that counts connected patches where one pattern's wall is at least 0.2 of full density. In our check (128 x 128, seeds 13 to 16) of the world where each pattern has its own phage, those patches were crests of moving waves, not closed bodies: 2 to 3 in 100 were still there 1,000 steps later, and those had moved well away from where they were. With phage switched off, almost none were still there. With one wall pattern only, about 1 in 5 were, as separate patches that move and do not last. The world where phage ignores the pattern was not checked.

A phage is a virus-like particle that can only grow in wall of one pattern, and it turns that wall back into loose pieces and more phage as it grows. It appears by rare mutation, drifts through the dish, and fades away when its pattern is gone. The idea being tested is that a pattern which spreads becomes an easy target, so another pattern takes over. Everything is counted in whole pieces: loose pieces, food, wall and phage together are one total that never changes, and the check below adds it all up exactly.

Designed ingredients: the way wall matter separates, the light that turns loose pieces into food, food building wall where wall already is, wall slowly falling back to loose pieces, and the phage rule are all written in. What is not written in is where the patches of wall are, how many there are, or which pattern leads.

What this has shown so far (docs/oligomer-2026-11-20/PHASE-B2-GPU.md, runs on a graphics card, 128 x 128 squares, 20,000 steps, 4 seeds per world; "late" means the last 60 percent of the run): with phage, the leading pattern changed 6 to 9 times (counting changes among patterns holding at least 25 percent of the wall); with no phage, 0 to 2 times; with phage that attacks any pattern, 1 to 9 times but with only 10 to 19 wall patches. Late patch counts: 65 to 70 with phage, 59 to 65 without, 17 to 25 with one wall pattern only. One 256 x 256 run of 10,000 steps held about 287 wall patches late. That table was run before a later fix to the kernel (a noise-arithmetic overflow, named in the doc's own spot-check). Our re-run on the current code (128 x 128 squares, 20,000 steps, 4 seeds) held 68 to 71 wall patches late with phage, 60 to 68 without, 19 to 29 with one wall pattern only and 4 to 15 with phage that attacks any pattern, and the leading pattern changed 7 to 10 times with phage, 0 to 1 without and 1 to 11 with phage that attacks any pattern. The 256 x 256 figure was not re-run. A patch count measures how much wall is above 0.2 and how finely it is cut. It is not a number of individuals. These are the designers' own experiments, not independent replications. The 32, 48 and 64 square sizes offered for the processor have no results of their own, and the leader-change counter on this page samples every 500 steps from step 2,000, so its numbers are not the same measurement as the table.

Our own test runs of this page's default world (128 x 128, seeds 1 to 3, counted in steps): the patch count passed 1.5 times its starting number by step 200 and peaked near step 400 to 500, and with phage the leading pattern first changed between steps 3,500 and 5,000. On the 48 x 48 processor default the first change came between steps 2,500 and 5,500, and with no phage the leader also changed (2 to 10 times by step 20,000), so a change on the processor grid is not evidence of phage.

      Counts, diversity and leader changes are read by observers that watch the run; they never change it. Known limits: the grid is flat and wraps around at the edges, matter only moves between neighbours, compartments drift by swapping places with a neighbour, and the viewer shows the leading pattern of each square, so a square that is half one pattern and half another looks like the larger half. Squares are not tracked as individuals: the inspector reads the square, not the cell that happens to sit in it. Death counts are observations, not causes: a death with no neighbour cut can be hunger, thin walls or random rupture, and a death with a neighbour cut means a lysin cut a neighbour during that step. The predator ring marks carriers of a lysin gene, not the cuts themselves. Results above are provisional.

      Known limits: the grid is flat and wraps around; the patch count is connected patches of at least three squares, each at 0.2 of full wall density or more in one pattern, so a patch holding two patterns is counted once per pattern; in our check of three of these worlds at 128 x 128, in the windows read from step 60,000 on, the typical peak on a patch was about a quarter of full density and no square of a patch passed half (patches are seeded denser than that); pattern shares are over all wall matter, including the thin wall outside the patches; a patch is not a tracked individual, and in our check of the world where each pattern has its own phage the patches were crests of moving waves, with 2 to 3 in 100 still there 1,000 steps later; phage and wall are numbers per square, not particles. The processor and graphics card engines give the same numbers bit for bit on the five cases in the golden test, which is a test of those cases, not a proof for every run. Inspecting never changes the run.
