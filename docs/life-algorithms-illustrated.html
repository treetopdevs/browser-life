01 / The big picture

A digital pond made of rules

Imagine a shallow pond drawn on graph paper. Every square holds amounts of nutrient, biomass, waste, structural material, and energy. Some squares also carry a tiny inherited recipe that controls what happens there.

On each tick, the program measures nearby material, moves some of it, spreads dissolved substances, and runs chemical reactions. Repeat this many times and patches can persist, move, grow, break apart, or disappear. A patch that looks like a creature is a pattern in the material, continuously maintaining itself.

Conceptual diagram. The last box is a question tested by experiments, not an automatic consequence of the first two.

Emergence means a large-scale behaviour arises from smaller interactions. A traffic jam emerges from drivers braking; nobody needs to command “make a jam.” Here, candidate bodies emerge from chemistry and material flow. The base rules do not contain a command that says “spawn a baby organism.”

Two meanings of “cell.” A grid cell is one square of the numerical world. It is not automatically a biological cell. A visible body usually spans many squares. Later experimental modes add explicit cell identities; those are explained separately.

This is an abstract artificial-life model, not a molecular simulation of real biology. “Nutrient,” “membrane,” and “photosynthesis” name useful roles in its rules. They are not literal proteins, atoms, or chloroplasts.

01B / A bridge from Conway’s Game of Life

Start with blinking squares. Then add an economy.

Conway’s Game of Life shows how a tiny rulebook can produce surprisingly organised patterns. Browser Life shares that idea, but asks what happens when patterns must also obtain resources, pay energy costs, and pass on inherited behaviour.

First, Conway’s entire rulebook

Imagine a grid where each square is either alive or dead. Each square counts its eight neighbours: above, below, left, right, and the four diagonals. Every square updates simultaneously using the old generation:

0 or 1 neighbourA live square dies: too few neighbours.
2 or 3 neighboursA live square stays alive.
4–8 neighboursA live square dies: too many neighbours.
Exactly 3 neighboursA dead square becomes alive. Otherwise it stays dead.

Those are the standard B3/S23 rules: birth with 3 neighbours; survival with 2 or 3. The words “birth” and “death” describe switches between two states. There is no food, energy balance, genome, or separate creature object in this rulebook.

Try the actual Conway rules

Generation 0 · 3 live squares · Blinker

A real, small Game of Life demonstration, not the Browser Life engine. This 12 × 12 board wraps at its edges; the mathematical Game of Life is usually defined on an infinite grid. Filled squares are alive; outlined empty squares are dead.

Watch a pattern, not a moving object

The blinker switches between a horizontal and a vertical row of three live squares. In the horizontal row, the centre has two neighbours and survives. Each end has only one and dies. The dead square above the centre and the one below it each have three live neighbours, so they turn on. After two generations, the original pattern returns.

The glider changes shape over four generations, then reappears shifted one square diagonally. No rule says “move this glider.” Individual squares turn on and off; the pattern travels.

This is the key connection to Browser Life: recognisable objects can be patterns produced by local updates. The observer names the object after seeing its behaviour.

Neighbor count becomes a neighbourhood measurement

Conway asks: “How many of my eight neighbours are alive?” Browser Life’s affinity pass asks: “How much bound material is around me, using my sampling stencil, and how close is that to the density my genome favours?” Its μ and σ play the role of a tunable density preference. This is an analogy, not a substitution of B3/S23 with a new birth rule: affinity steers material flow; chemistry actually makes biomass.

An on/off birth becomes a paid conversion

In Conway, three neighbours are enough to turn an empty square on. No other square must give up matter to pay for that birth. In Browser Life, a growing patch must turn existing A into B and account for the energy. If it spends its free energy or blocks its own nutrient supply, its inherited strategy may fail even if its shape looks stable.

Conway: neighbourhood → next on/off pattern
Browser Life: neighbourhood + local genome + resources → movement and reactions → next resource pattern

A glider is a useful analogy—with a limit

A glider demonstrates movement emerging from simple rules. It does not have to feed, repair itself, or reproduce with variation to keep moving through empty space. A Browser Life patch faces maintenance, decay, and resource constraints. A convincing “living” pattern therefore needs evidence beyond recognisable motion.

Likewise, a Conway glider gun can repeatedly emit gliders, but emission alone is not the same as an ecology undergoing heritable adaptation. Complex computation and elaborate constructions are possible in Conway; the distinction here is that its basic rulebook does not include Browser Life’s resource ledger or local mutable genomes.

In both, a pattern is a higher-level description of activity on the grid. Browser Life adds interacting subsystems inside each location, and a research supersystem outside the world that tests which life-like claims are justified. It is best understood as another local-rule artificial-life model, not Conway’s rules with prettier graphics.

Does randomness break the comparison?

Conway’s next generation is completely determined by the current grid. Browser Life also has a reproducible next state when the full state, configuration, step and seed are fixed. Its lotteries and stochastic rounding use repeatable random draws. Changing the seed can change the history; rerunning the same valid history should not.

02 / Supersystems and subsystems

Zoom from the research project to one reaction

A supersystem is the larger system something belongs to. A subsystem is one of its working parts. The same thing can be both: a world contains chemistry, while an experiment contains worlds.

Research program

Asks whether local rules can produce self-maintenance, heredity, a recycling ecology, and higher-level reproducing groups. Experiments and controls supply evidence; an attractive animation alone cannot answer these questions.

Select a level to inspect its job and connections. This is a conceptual hierarchy: bodies and lineages overlap rather than fitting into perfectly separate boxes.

There are really two maps

The ecological map describes the simulated world. The computing map describes the machinery running it. A volunteer browser called an “island” is a worker computer, while a world tile is a simulated habitat. Moving a computation between machines does not itself move any simulated biomass.

The square also stores MOT, a packed pair of movement outputs, and a genome: the local inherited recipe. MOT is control information; it is neither matter nor an energy reserve.

Total matter = sum over every square of (A + B + C + P)

The total stays fixed while the base world runs without external matter edits. Feed and Drain deliberately change that total and are logged; Wound converts B and P into C rather than removing matter. Making biomass means converting existing nutrient into biomass. It does not create matter. E and S are counted in the energy ledger instead.

The edges join up

Each tile wraps left to right and top to bottom, like the map in a game where leaving one edge brings you back at the opposite edge. Mathematicians call this a torus. There is no wall at a tile edge. Different tiles remain independent unless an explicit migration or other experimental rule connects them.

04 / The algorithm's heartbeat

One tick has four main passes

A tick is a simulation step, not necessarily one video frame or a fixed amount of real time. The reference implementation runs these passes in this order:

1. AffinityMeasure the surrounding pattern.
2. FlowCalculate where material tends to move.
3. TransportMove amounts and choose inherited identities.
4. ReactRead sensors, run chemistry, pay costs, mutate and decay.

All locations follow the same rulebook, but their surroundings and genomes differ. A sunny, nutrient-rich square can therefore behave differently from a crowded, dark one.

A subtle timing detail: movement uses the stored MOT output from the previous reaction pass. The controller calculates a new movement output during this tick’s reactions, ready for the next tick. Its affinity input comes from this tick’s earlier affinity pass.

Why separate passes instead of updating one whole square at a time?

If the first square immediately changed what the second square read, processing order could change the result. Separate passes and input/output buffers make the timing explicit. The GPU can calculate many destinations in parallel without letting whichever thread happens to finish first redefine the physics.

05 / Shape and movement

A local preference creates a landscape

For each location, the algorithm looks at nearby bound material M = B + P. A ring-shaped weighting pattern, called a kernel, decides how much each neighbour contributes. Think of it as a stencil for measuring the neighbourhood.

Schematic, not a screenshot or the exact kernel tap weights.

The resulting density is compared with two inherited parameters:

  • μ (mu): the preferred neighbourhood density.
  • σ (sigma): the width of that preference.

The output, U, is an affinity score. Near the preferred density, U is high. Far away, U is low. “Preference” here means a numerical rule, not a conscious desire.

Explore the affinity curve
Teaching plot: the smooth polynomial behind the integer growth function, using illustrative density values. The real function rounds at intermediate steps. This curve changes movement preference; it does not create biomass.

A gradient is a direction of increase, like uphill on a map. Flow combines a tendency to climb the affinity landscape with a tendency to move away from crowding. As local mass increases, the crowding term gets more weight. A Sobel stencil estimates these gradients from neighbouring values.

Movement ≈ attraction toward higher affinity − crowding pressure + active motility

The inherited controller can add its own x/y movement bias. An optional adhesion rule adds attraction toward structural polymer; it is off by default. The code limits displacement so material cannot jump arbitrarily far in one tick. This is a designed local flow law, not a simulation of muscles or Newtonian rigid bodies.

06 / Transport and inheritance

Move the material; take a recipe along

Moving material is trickier than moving a dot on a screen. A source square’s B, P, and E are spread across destination squares according to how a displaced box overlaps them. This is called reintegration: redistribute a continuous-looking movement onto a discrete grid.

No lost crumbs

Suppose 11 units need to split equally across four destinations. Rounding each share down gives 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8. The remaining 3 units stay at the source. Nothing disappears. Real shares depend on geometric overlap, but the same remainder rule applies.

When recipes meet, one wins locally

A destination may receive bound mass from several lineages, but it stores only one genome. The base rule chooses that genome using a lottery weighted by incoming B + P. It does not average the genomes or perform sexual recombination.

A two-source genome lottery
70%30%

Blue brings 70 of 100 bound-mass units, so its genome has a 70% chance of winning this destination. Purple has 30%.

An idealized probability illustration. A real destination can gather contributions from up to nine source squares. All incoming base-rule material remains even though only one recipe wins.

This matters scientifically. A genome can gain control over material made by other genomes. Spreading an inherited recipe is therefore not automatically the same as reproducing a whole self-contained body.

Dissolved substances take another route

A, C, and S exchange with four immediate neighbours. This is diffusion: many small transfers tend to smooth differences. Polymer P slows the exchange of A and C using the factor below. Signal S is not membrane-gated by this rule.

Exchange multiplier = gateK / (gateK + polymer at source + polymer at destination)

More polymer means a smaller multiplier. Structure can help retain substances, but it can also restrict fresh nutrient supply or waste removal. That is a tradeoff, not a free protective wall.

07 / The inherited controller

A tiny calculator at every occupied location

The genome carries a fixed-size neural network: 10 inputs → 8 hidden values → 8 outputs. “Neural network” sounds complicated, but each stage mostly multiplies inputs by inherited weights, adds them together, and clips the answer to a permitted range.

Actual network dimensions and sensor/output categories. Sensors are scaled and capped integers, not raw unbounded amounts.

Imagine one hidden value responding strongly to light and another to low energy. Their connections could make photosynthesis stronger in sunlight and respiration stronger when the energy balance is low. The hand-built generalist genome uses rules of this kind, but other genomes can wire the same sensors very differently.

The chemistry outputs use only their positive portion. A negative photosynthesis output means zero requested catalysis; it does not reverse the reaction. Movement outputs are signed so they can point in either direction.

Hidden value = clip((bias × 128 + Σ(weight × input)) ÷ 128, 0, 127)

Σ simply means “add all the terms.” For the output stage, the same weighted-sum idea clips to −127…127. Integer division and the clipping rules are part of the simulation.

What is in the genome?

There are 44 words, each 32 bits: two words for lineage identity, two for growth parameters and movement gain, and 40 packing 160 signed bytes. Those 160 values include weights and biases: 80 input-to-hidden weights + 8 hidden biases + 64 hidden-to-output weights + 8 output biases.

Evolution changes the recipe; experience does not train it with backpropagation. The controller responds to current sensors, but the base simulation does not run a learning optimizer on each body. Changes in inherited parameters come from mutation and spread through the ecology.

08 / Metabolism and the energy budget

Every useful conversion has a cost or a source

The chemistry runs in a fixed order. Each reaction is limited by the resources present, its configured rate, the controller output, and the amount of effective catalyst. A strong output is permission to try—not a guarantee that resources exist.

1 · Photosynthesis
A + light → B

Turns nutrient into biomass. Absorbed light pays for the increase in chemical energy.

2 · Respiration
B → C + E

Breaks biomass into waste and releases spendable energy.

3 · Decomposition
C → A + E

Recycles waste into nutrient, releasing the configured chemical-energy difference. This may be zero when A and C have equal potential energy.

4 · Growth
A + E → B

Builds biomass by spending saved energy instead of directly absorbing light.

5 · Polymer building
B + E → P

Converts active material into structure, paying the extra chemical-energy cost.

6 · Signal emission
E → S

Moves free energy into the diffusing signal pool. Communication is not free.

A tiny patch has a harder time getting started

The effective catalyst is approximately B² / (B + K), where K is a configured scale. With little biomass, activity is disproportionately weak. With lots of biomass, it approaches proportionality to B. This is an Allee effect: being extremely small is a disadvantage.

For example, with K = 100, B = 10 gives about 0.91 effective units, while B = 100 gives 50. These are smooth explanatory values; the executable uses an integer form. This mechanism helps distinguish a substantial self-maintaining patch from scattered traces of biomass.

Then the bill arrives

The code charges for requested catalytic activity and maintenance. If E covers the bill, that energy leaves as heat. If E runs out, some biomass becomes waste through starvation. Polymer and biomass also decay, E leaks, S decays, and a light-dependent abiotic process recycles some C into A. “Abiotic” means it does not require living biomass to catalyze it.

Mutation is checked after the work/maintenance stage and before passive decays in the current implementation. It uses the biomass synthesised earlier in this reaction pass.

The species have configured energy levels with eA ≤ eC < eB < eP. Going upward costs energy; going downward releases it. Pool caps export excess energy as heat, so overflow cannot secretly create or erase energy. This is exact resource accounting, not a calibrated thermodynamic model of Earth.

09 / Heredity, mutation, selection

Evolution has no foresight

The base system combines three ingredients: recipes travel with material, newly synthesised biomass can trigger mutation, and different recipes spread or persist differently under the same local rules.

VariationA parameter changes locally.
InheritanceTransport copies the winning genome.
ConsequencesMetabolism and movement change.
SelectionSome variants leave more persistent material carrying their recipe.

During a mutation event, one controller byte, μ, σ, or movement gain is perturbed and clamped to its legal range. Optional heritable-shape modes add ring-weight slots. The changed location receives a new lineage ID, and a parent–child lineage event is recorded.

The trigger scales with the amount of new B made through photosynthesis and growth, with a cap. The base synthesis-mutation rule permits at most one mutation event per grid location per step; it does not separately mutate every newly made quantum. A clamped perturbation can leave the inherited values unchanged even though the event mints a new lineage identity. Transport by itself does not trigger mutation.

A useful feedback loop

Genome → local behaviour → material and energy distribution → survival and spread → which genomes are common → local behaviour again

Suppose a variant spends more on polymer. It may retain resources better, but grow more slowly. Whether it spreads depends on light, neighbours, diffusion, and costs. There is no universal “best genome,” and no built-in score rewarding the base ecology for looking more complex.

Chance also matters. A genome can win a transport lottery without being better. This is why researchers compare many seeds and use neutral controls rather than interpreting every change as adaptation.

10 / From patches to ecology

The observer asks: “Was that a birth?”

The base physics handles amounts and local genomes. A separate measurement subsystem, the census, finds patches connected through four edge-sharing neighbours, wrapping within each tile. By default it includes squares with B + P ≥ 48; the individuals filter then retains components with total bound mass ≥ 256. Diagonal contact alone does not connect two patches.

The tracker compares these patches across observations using overlap. It can label births, deaths, fissions, and fusions. A fission means that a previously connected patch is now associated with multiple sufficiently large patches. It is a detected event, not a command sent to the physics.

One patch (left) becomes two candidate descendants (right). Schematic of a fission interpretation, not a recorded simulation result. Thresholds and observation timing affect what the tracker sees.

Detection alone is not proof of biological reproduction. Researchers must ask whether descendants inherit traits, survive independently, regrow after damage, and maintain a meaningful causal connection to the parent. Two dots with the same genome could instead reflect a recipe spreading through shared material.

How an ecology could appear

A lineage doing much photosynthesis can supply biomass; another reaction pattern can release waste; decomposition can return waste to nutrient. Diffusion and transport connect these local processes into resource cycles. Roles are inferred from measured reaction activity, not assigned as “plant,” “predator,” or “decomposer” classes.

In particular, B → C acts on local biomass. There is no base-rule hunting command or separate prey object. Interactions arise through shared resources, movement, mixing, and local chemistry.

What would a larger living unit require?

A cluster of bodies moving together is a candidate collective. To argue that it has become a new reproducing unit, one would need stronger evidence: a group produces descendants, a small founding packet rebuilds the group’s organisation, and group properties are inherited. These are research targets, not guarantees of the algorithm.

11 / The experimental supersystem

How do we tell an interesting movie from a result?

The project includes machinery around the ecology to choose starting conditions, run comparisons, and measure outcomes. These outer algorithms must not be confused with the world’s local physics.

Founder search: stock the starting pond

The bootstrap uses MAP-Elites, a search method that keeps a collection of useful candidates across different behaviour categories. Here, the archive uses categories based on mass and speed. Evaluations grow candidates, injure them, and test them without light; screening and retesting look for viable, self-maintaining founders.

This is human-designed selection during preparation. The founder-search optimizer is switched off in ensemble runs. It is different from a mutation succeeding or failing inside an ongoing ecology. Starting with selected founders also means the experiment is not claiming spontaneous origin of life from bare nutrient.

This guide explains mechanisms, not a declaration of success. Persistence, regeneration, heredity, ecological diversity, and open-ended evolution are separate claims. Implementing a mechanism that permits one does not prove that a run achieves it.

12 / The computing supersystem

Many machines, one repeatable rulebook

CPU: the readable specification

The TypeScript reference implements the rules explicitly. It is the clearest place to follow the arithmetic and serves as the comparison target.

GPU: thousands of local calculations

WebGPU shaders perform the same work in parallel. They must produce the same integer state, bit for bit. Golden cases check the implementations against each other.

Fixed point represents fractions using scaled integers. For example, storing 128 on a scale of 256 represents one half. This avoids depending on small differences in floating-point behaviour across devices.

Counter-based randomness is repeatable randomness: a draw is determined by the seed, step, location, and draw purpose. Asking for that same draw again produces the same answer. A GPU thread finishing early does not consume someone else’s random number.

Stochastic rounding handles small expected amounts fairly. If a calculation gives 2.3, it can yield 3 with 30% probability and 2 otherwise. Over many draws the average is 2.3. In this simulation those draws are deterministic for a given history. Transport’s retained remainders are a separate conservation mechanism.

The distributed workflow

AssignThe coordinator leases a run segment.
ComputeA browser or Deno worker advances the world.
SavePhysics and observer state enter a checkpoint.
VerifySelected segments are replayed and hashes compared.

A hash is a compact fingerprint of data. Matching end-state hashes support agreement between replays. A checkpoint includes observer state as well as physics so continuing a segmented run can preserve the measurement history too.

The lab adds a presentation subsystem: its worker owns the GPU and frame loop, while execution code coordinates observations and replay. Rendering turns numbers into colours. Those colours are a view of the state; changing a display layer does not create a new chemical species.

14 / Put it all together

Follow one patch through a sunny day

  1. It has a neighbourhood. Nearby B + P produces a ring-weighted density. The patch’s inherited μ and σ turn that into affinity.
  2. It moves and mixes. Flow balances affinity, crowding, and stored motility. Material is redistributed, dissolved resources diffuse, and each destination picks its genome.
  3. It senses its new local situation. The controller reads chemistry, light, energy per biomass, signal and signal slopes, plus the earlier affinity field.
  4. It spends and transforms. Photosynthesis may convert A to B. Respiration may refill E. Growth and polymer construction spend E. Emission puts some E into S.
  5. It pays for being active. Work and maintenance export heat. Shortages can destroy biomass; passive processes continue even without active catalysis.
  6. A recipe may change. Synthesized biomass can trigger a local mutation. If that variant spreads, later neighbourhoods may behave differently.
  7. An observer checks the outcome. At census times, dense connected regions are measured and tracked. Did the patch recover, split, fuse, or vanish? Further experiments determine what those events mean.

The whole system is a loop: local rules build an environment, the environment changes which inherited behaviours persist, and those behaviours reshape the environment.

Check your understanding

If biomass doubles, did matter double? No. A or another matter pool must have decreased through the permitted conversions.

If two bodies share a lineage, are they one body? No. Lineage identity and spatial connectedness are different groupings.

If a body splits, has the system proved reproduction? The tracker has detected a candidate event. Heredity, viability, and causal continuity still need testing.

If a network output is high, must the reaction happen strongly? No. Substrate, energy, rate constants, and catalyst can limit it.

If one worker computes faster, should it evolve a different world? Given the same valid physics and observer state, configuration, seed, and intervention or exchange schedule, it should reach the same state after the same steps.
