# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Curious people who want to watch artificial life grow are the primary audience (since 2026-10-04, when the project chose bigger, exploratory bets over defensible endpoints). They plant worlds, poke them, come back to see what changed and bring back a good "what is that?"; most were not there when a world was built, so every choice is explained in their words. Researchers, the project's own runs included, are the second audience: they set up worlds, inspect local state, and judge whether a run's measurements and replay evidence are trustworthy, so that evidence stays close to the world. The public site introduces the garden and opens the lab; the lab remains the primary surface.

## Product Purpose

Cadence Garden is a browser-hosted research lab for an open-ended artificial-life ecology. It lets researchers interact with simulations governed by exact integer physics, inspect emerging behavior, and retain evidence through metrics, checkpoints, and replay checks. Success means a visitor or a researcher can grow a world, understand what happened, and tell an observation from a verified result.

## Brand Personality

An experimental creative studio with scientific credibility: curious, inventive, and precise, and friendly about it. The frame is a garden (2026-10-06): every world is a sealed jar under a lamp, the lab is the bench it stands on, and the site is the garden around it. The voice is plain and warm, utilitarian in the manner of Ableton Live and classic Facebook: controls say what they do in ordinary words, panels are titled with the question they answer, and nothing hides behind jargon. The interface should invite exploration while remaining clear about controls, measurements, and uncertainty.

## Anti-references

Avoid toy-like styling that trivializes the research and museum-site styling that turns the simulation into a passive exhibit. Avoid presentation that makes a visual pattern look like scientific proof.

## Design Principles

1. Keep the living world central to the task.
2. Make experiment controls explicit and predictable: say what a control will do before it is used, and what it did afterwards.
3. Put measurements and integrity evidence close to the observations they explain.
4. Reward exploration without overstating what a run demonstrates.
5. Let the public-facing surface grow from the lab's real work and results.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Support keyboard operation, readable contrast, and reduced-motion preferences. Do not rely on color alone to communicate simulation state or verification outcomes.
